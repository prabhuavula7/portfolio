## Context

In March 2026, Emergence Capital published what they called an AI-Native Services Playbook, a framework for a category of company they believe will define the AI era. The same week, Harvey hit an $11 billion valuation on $190M ARR from serving legal teams at over 1,300 firms. Harper, an AI-native commercial insurance brokerage out of YC W25, closed a $47M raise to keep scaling a workflow that traditionally took human brokers five to seven days per client.

These companies are not SaaS businesses with AI features. They're not agencies using AI tools to work faster. They're something structurally different: companies where the AI system executes the service end to end, owns the customer relationship, and generates a data flywheel that gets harder to compete with every quarter. The distinction matters because the architecture is completely different from anything that came before it, and most engineers building today are still defaulting to the SaaS mental model when they should be rethinking the whole stack.

I've spent the last several months writing about the technical internals of agentic systems: context management as infrastructure, async pipeline design, where to draw the autonomy boundary. This is the view from one level up. What does it actually look like when you build a company on top of those patterns instead of a feature? What's different about the architecture when the AI doesn't just assist the work — it IS the work?

---

## The three tiers, and why only one of them has a real moat

When people say they're building an "AI company," they usually mean one of three structurally different things. The differences matter a lot more than the terminology suggests.

```text
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    THREE TIERS OF AI IN PRODUCTS                    │
├───────────────────┬──────────────────────┬──────────────────────────┤
│   AI-ASSISTED     │   AI-AUGMENTED       │   AI-NATIVE SERVICES     │
├───────────────────┼──────────────────────┼──────────────────────────┤
│                   │                      │                          │
│  Human ──► Tool   │  Human ──► Agent     │  Customer defines goal   │
│  Tool ──► Output  │  Agent ──► Human     │         │                │
│                   │  Human ──► Output    │         ▼                │
│  Human drives     │                      │  AI executes end-to-end  │
│  everything.      │  Human still gates   │         │                │
│  AI speeds it up. │  every step.         │         ▼                │
│                   │  AI handles parts.   │  Human reviews at gates  │
│                   │                      │         │                │
│  Examples:        │  Examples:           │         ▼                │
│  Copilot, Grammarly│ AI-powered CRM,    │  Outcome delivered       │
│  smart search     │  AI writing assist   │         │                │
│                   │                      │         ▼                │
│                   │                      │  Data feeds next run     │
│                   │                      │                          │
│  Moat: UX         │  Moat: Integration   │  Moat: Data flywheel     │
└───────────────────┴──────────────────────┴──────────────────────────┘
```

**AI-Assisted** is where most enterprise software lives today. AI gives humans better tools: autocomplete, smart search, document summaries. The human drives every decision. The AI speeds things up. The moat, such as it is, comes from UX and switching costs. As models commoditize, the marginal value of any individual AI-assisted feature compresses fast.

**AI-Augmented** is where most "AI-powered" SaaS features land. The model handles contained subtasks while humans orchestrate the overall process. The AI drafts a contract section; the lawyer reviews and edits. The AI generates a quote; the broker approves and sends it. This is useful. It is not structurally different enough from the tier below it to defend a business on long term.

**AI-Native Services** is the structurally distinct category. The AI executes the work end to end. Humans define the goal and review consequences at key gates. The company is accountable for the outcome, not the tool. And critically: the data generated by executing the work feeds back into the system, improving every subsequent run. The moat is not the model. It's the flywheel the model runs on.

Most of the AI investment and hype in 2024 and 2025 went into tier one and tier two: better copilots, smarter assistants, AI features bolted onto existing SaaS. The interesting action in 2026 is tier three, and it's moving faster than the commentary around it.

## What the architecture actually requires

You can't build a tier-three company by adding AI to a SaaS architecture. The execution model is different in kind, and the architecture has to be designed from the start to support autonomous, long-running pipelines that generate and consume structured data at scale.

There are three architectural primitives that separate AI-native services companies from everything else.

### Async pipelines as the core product

In SaaS, the user interacts with the product in real time. The interface is the product. In an AI-native services company, the user submits a goal and the system executes it, which might take minutes, hours, or days. The "product" is not a screen; it's an execution engine that delivers a finished output. This requires a completely different product mental model: not flows and modals, but queues, dashboards, and structured deliverables.

```text
TRADITIONAL SAAS                      AI-NATIVE SERVICES
────────────────────────              ────────────────────────────────
User ──► UI Interaction               Customer ──► Goal Submission
UI ──► Immediate Response                          │
User ──► Next Action                               ▼
UI ──► Immediate Response             Async Pipeline Executes
...repeat until done                               │
                                       ┌───────────┴──────────────┐
Human drives every step.               │                          │
AI is advisory.                        ▼                          ▼
                                  Research Agent            Data Captured
                                  (full autonomy)                 │
                                       │                          ▼
                                       ▼                    Model Improves
                                  Human Review Gate
                                  (one gate, at consequence)
                                       │
                                       ▼
                                  Action Agent Executes
                                       │
                                       ▼
                                  Outcome Delivered
```

The pipeline design determines the product experience. Customers don't use the system in real time; they submit work and come back to finished artifacts. This is a more demanding bar to clear than a chatbot, and it's a much harder bar to replicate once the pipeline is tuned to a specific vertical.

### The data flywheel as the actual moat

Here's the thing about moats in AI that I think is underappreciated: the model is not the moat. The models are commoditizing. GPT-5, Claude 4, Gemini, all of them are available to anyone on the same API at roughly similar price points. Any feature built on top of a foundation model is one API call away from being replicated by a competitor.

The flywheel is the moat. In AI-native services, the data generated by doing the work feeds directly back into the system, making every subsequent run faster, more accurate, and cheaper to execute. This is not data as a byproduct. It's data as the core product investment.

```text
                    ┌──────────────────────────────┐
                    │       Customer Goal           │
                    └──────────────┬───────────────┘
                                   │
                                   ▼
                    ┌──────────────────────────────┐
                    │    AI Executes the Work       │
                    └──────────┬────────┬──────────┘
                               │        │
                    ┌──────────▼──┐  ┌──▼────────────────────┐
                    │  Outcome    │  │  Operational Data      │
                    │  Delivered  │  │  (signals, outcomes,   │
                    └─────────────┘  │   edge cases, errors)  │
                                     └──────────┬─────────────┘
                                                │
                                                ▼
                                  ┌─────────────────────────┐
                                  │  Model and System       │
                                  │  Improves               │
                                  └─────────────┬───────────┘
                                                │
                                                ▼
                                  ┌─────────────────────────┐
                                  │  Next Run Is Better,    │◄── compounding
                                  │  Faster, and Cheaper    │
                                  └─────────────────────────┘
```

Emergence Capital puts this clearly in their playbook: "Every engagement should make your AI better, your delivery faster, and your outcomes more predictable." Harper illustrates it specifically: every lead, call, email, and policy generates data that feeds their carrier appetite model, improving matching between businesses and underwriters and driving higher conversion rates. That model is not available on any API. It was built one placement at a time.

### The autonomy boundary as product design

Every AI-native services company has to make an explicit decision about where humans stay in the loop and where the AI operates with full autonomy. This is not a safety setting. It's the most consequential product design decision in the business.

The failure modes are symmetric. Over-gate every action and you've built an approval queue: the AI does fragments of work while humans review and click approve at every step. Customers signed up for leverage; they're getting a very expensive interface for doing their own job in smaller pieces. Under-gate and you've built something nobody trusts in production. Users run it once on real data, watch something go sideways, and don't come back.

The pattern that works: gate at the irreversibility boundary. Research, analysis, synthesis, and structuring can run with full AI autonomy. These are read-only, reversible, and reviewable. The gate goes before outbound actions, written records, client-facing outputs, or anything consequential that can't be undone. One human checkpoint, at the right moment, rather than twenty checkpoints at every step.

## Mirage PMF: the failure mode nobody talks about loudly enough

Emergence introduced a term in their playbook that captures something I've seen in a lot of "AI companies" and hadn't had precise language for: Mirage PMF. The illusion of product-market fit created by revenue growth powered by human labor, not AI leverage.

The warning signs are specific: gross margin stays flat even as revenue grows, headcount grows linearly with customer count, each new customer requires significant custom work, delivery is still mostly human, and there's no north star metric that tracks how much of the work AI is actually doing.

These are signs that you've built a competent services firm financed with venture capital. That's not a bad business, but it's not the business the pitch deck described.

The test is simple: is revenue per employee improving over time? If not, the AI is not pulling its weight at scale. You're billing for hours with a better story around them.

One useful north star metric for this: Human Review Time, the minutes of human labor required per unit of work after AI processing, without sacrificing quality. As that number approaches zero, margins approach software margins. Every AI-native services company needs their own version of it. If you can't define yours, you probably have Mirage PMF.

## The companies building this right

**Harvey** is the clearest large-scale proof point. At $190M ARR, 100,000+ lawyers across 1,300 organizations, and more than 25,000 custom agents handling M&A due diligence, contract drafting, and document review, they've built the data flywheel that matters in legal: judgment accumulated across every major law firm type, deal structure, and document category. A $200M raise at an $11B valuation in March 2026 suggests investors believe the flywheel is working.

**Decagon** applies the same pattern to customer service. Their AI concierge handles full customer interactions, escalates when needed, and continuously improves from every conversation. $35M ARR by October 2025, $250M Series D at a $4.5B valuation in January 2026. The data compounding in customer service is less about domain expertise and more about conversational patterns: every resolved ticket teaches the system something about how a specific customer base communicates, what escalation signals look like, and where the edge cases live.

**Harper** is the insurance brokerage version and worth understanding in some detail because the flywheel is particularly visible. They don't sell software to brokers; they ARE the broker, licensed and operating. Their AI handles intake, submission assembly, carrier matching, underwriter follow-up, and quote comparison across 160+ carriers. A traditional brokerage manages 20 to 30 deals a month per person. Harper handles 1,000+ customers a month. The data from every placement feeds a carrier appetite model that reflects pricing patterns, acceptance rates, and underwriter behavior in a way that no individual broker relationship could accumulate. In 13 months, they've served 5,000+ businesses. That's a lot of flywheel turns.

## Why the model works where SaaS couldn't

Traditional SaaS companies have a structural attribution problem with AI: if a lawyer uses Harvey to draft a contract section, how much value did Harvey add versus the lawyer's judgment? This makes outcome-based pricing complicated and limits how much value you can capture.

AI-native services companies don't have this problem. They ARE the service. The customer hired them to deliver an outcome, and they're accountable for delivering it. There's no attribution challenge because there's no human doing the work on the customer's side. This makes outcome-based pricing the natural model: pay per placement, pay per document reviewed, pay per claim processed.

That's a different economic structure from per-seat SaaS, and it scales differently. As the AI improves and handles more volume per unit of compute, margins expand. Customer count grows without proportional headcount growth. Revenue per employee becomes the metric that actually matters.

Emergence frames the pricing evolution clearly: start with labor-based pricing because that's what the market understands, but set a clear timeline to transition to outcome-based pricing as the delivery model stabilizes. Companies that stay on labor-based pricing too long end up compressing their own margins as automation improves. The whole point of the flywheel is that the cost to deliver goes down. You want pricing that captures the value of the outcome, not the diminishing cost of the labor.

## What this means for how we build

If you're building an AI product right now, the most useful question to ask is which tier your architecture actually supports, not which tier you're claiming to be in.

Tier one and tier two products have real value. Copilots are genuinely useful. AI-augmented workflows save meaningful time. But they face a structural ceiling: as the underlying models commoditize, the marginal value of any individual AI feature compresses. The model powering your feature is available to every competitor on the same API at the same price.

A tier-three product has a different ceiling. The models commoditize; the flywheel doesn't. The carrier appetite signal Harper has built from 5,000+ commercial placements across 160 carriers is not on any API. The legal pattern recognition Harvey has accumulated across 100,000 lawyers and a million documents is not available to download. These are compounding assets built by doing the work, one engagement at a time.

The architectural primitives that make tier three possible, async execution, data capture by design, explicit autonomy boundaries, durable pipeline orchestration, are available to anyone building today. The window to build the flywheel in a given vertical is not open forever. The teams that move first and operate with genuine AI leverage, not Mirage PMF, are the ones who will find it closed behind them when they look back.
